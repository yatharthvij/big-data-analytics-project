Return Risk Intelligence: Predicting Return Probability, Return Fraud & Product Quality Issues

A PySpark/MLlib solution that separates genuine returns, product-quality failures, and fraud/abuse at the point of return request — so a retailer's return-audit team can act on the 1-in-3 returns worth a second look, instead of reviewing all of them.

Consulting Report · E-commerce Domain
Dataset: ecommerce_return_abuse_dataset.csv (Kaggle) — 60,000 orders × 35 features

Executive Summary

Retailers processing high return volumes cannot scale spreadsheet-based manual audits. This project builds an end-to-end Big Data Analytics pipeline — architecture, feature engineering, and three independent PySpark MLlib models — that triages every return request into legitimate, policy abuse, fraudulent return, or wardrobing, and separately segments customers/returns by behavior to surface product-quality problems the fraud label alone can't see.

Five findings drive the recommendations:

  1. Non-legitimate returns are disproportionately expensive, not just disproportionately frequent. They're 29.9% of orders but 43.8% of refund dollars requested — fraudulent returns alone average $434.73 per claim vs. $141.14 for legitimate returns, a 3.1× gap. Bad actors aren't just more numerous, they're targeting higher-value claims.
  2. Fraud is classifiable with high confidence from behavioral and evidence signals (tracking validity, photo evidence, address changes, refund-to-different-account) combined into an engineered fraud_signal_score — a Random Forest model built on these features reaches nearly perfect separation on this dataset (see the model-evaluation caveat in Section 5.1 before reading that number as a production guarantee), and beats a free rule-based heuristic decisively (F1 1.00 vs. 0.61).
  3. Return fraud and product quality are largely separate problems that get conflated in a single "return rate" metric. Clustering on behavior alone (no fraud label) finds a 73%-of-customers "core legitimate" segment whose returns are still 38% quality-issue driven — this is a QA problem, not a fraud problem, and needs a different owner and fix. It is also a broad-based one: quality-issue rates band tightly (31.5%–35.2%) across every product category, not one bad vendor (Section 6).
  4. Refund exposure is close to linear in order value — a simple Linear Regression model prices expected refund dollars at the moment a return is filed, cutting error 40% versus a naive "refund = order value" guess, and beating a more complex GBTRegressor honestly (Section 5.3).
  5. A production system needs three different interventions, not one: automated fraud-score routing for the classifier's high-risk tail, policy tightening for the frequent-low-value-returner cluster, and a systemic (not single-vendor) quality escalation for the segment carrying the highest quality-issue rate.

1 · Business Context & Problem Framing

An e-commerce retailer's return-audit team currently reviews disputed or high-value returns manually. That does not scale: return volume grows faster than headcount, audit criteria are applied inconsistently across reviewers, and the team has no systematic way to tell whether a spike in returns for a product category is fraud, abuse of a lenient return policy, or a genuine quality defect that needs a vendor escalation — three problems that call for three different responses, currently indistinguishable in a spreadsheet.

Industry background

This is not a hypothetical problem. E-commerce retailers face structurally higher return burdens than brick-and-mortar: the National Retail Federation (NRF) forecasts consumers will return $849.9 billion in merchandise in 2025 — 15.8% of all retail purchases — with online sales specifically returned at 19.3%, well above the all-channel average.1 Return fraud and abuse compound this: Appriss Retail's 2024 industry study (60+ retailers, with Deloitte) found 15.14% of all returns were fraudulent, costing retailers $103 billion in 2024 alone, up from $101B in 2023.2 Wardrobing specifically — one of this project's four target classes — is flagged by 60% of retail executives as a major fraud pattern in that same study, and 85% of retailers now deploy AI to detect and prevent return fraud.2 This project sits squarely inside that industry-wide shift, not ahead of or apart from it.

How our dataset compares: this dataset's combined Fraudulent Return + Wardrobing rate (10.2% + 7.7% = 17.9%) sits close to Appriss's reported 15.14% industry-wide fraud rate — the class prevalence is realistically calibrated to real-world reporting, even though (per the caveat in Section 5.1) the separability of the classes is not.

Why this matters financially

Of 60,000 orders in the dataset, 17,940 (29.9%) are non-legitimate returns (Policy Abuser 12.0%, Fraudulent Return 10.2%, Wardrobing 7.7%). Every one of those returns today gets the same manual-review treatment as a genuine defective-product return — the highest-value lever is triage, not blanket policy tightening, which would punish the 70% of legitimate customers too.

The dollar exposure is concentrated, not evenly spread: non-legitimate returns account for $4.64M of the $10.57M in total refund dollars requested across the dataset — 43.8% of the money, from 29.9% of the orders. Fraudulent Return claims alone total $2.66M at an average $434.73 per claim — 3.1× the $141.14 average for legitimate returns. A further $1.88M sits in a single specific, rule-based pattern independent of any ML model: non-legitimate returns that are both a high-value item and missing photo evidence (4,673 orders) — an immediately actionable policy (require photo evidence above a value threshold) that doesn't need to wait for the classifier to ship. Figures computed directly from data/processed/silver; see Section 7 for how they translate into recommendations.

4 · Data Engineering (Phase 2)

Implemented in notebooks/01_data_engineering.ipynb, executed end-to-end against the full 60,000-row dataset with PySpark (not pandas/scikit-learn, per the course's distributed-computing requirement).

4.1 Data quality

Verified programmatically, not assumed: 0 nulls across all 35 columns, 0 duplicate order_id, 0 logical violations (no return-before-order dates, no returns-exceeding-orders, no implausible ages).

4.2 Outlier treatment

IQR-flagged outliers on 8 continuous fields were capped at the 1st/99th percentile rather than deleted. For a fraud dataset, extreme values are frequently the signal itself (an unusually large refund request, an unusually fast wishlist-to-cart flip); deletion would remove exactly the rows the model most needs.

4.3 Feature engineering

FeatureServesRationale
fraud_signal_scoreFraudAdditive count of 6 red-flags (address change, refund-to-different-account, multiple accounts, invalid tracking, no photo evidence, tampered packaging) — a single interpretable risk score
return_to_order_ratioReturn riskIndependently recomputed cross-check of the provided return_rate_pct
refund_to_order_value_ratioFraudRefund requested relative to order value — abuse/fraud skews this ratio
is_quality_issue_reasonQualityFlags Defective/Broken, Quality Issue, Not As Described — the slice QA owns vs. fraud

Validation, not assumption: mean fraud_signal_score is 2.73 for Fraudulent Return vs. 0.84 for Legitimate, and mean return_to_order_ratio is 0.47 vs. 0.05 — confirmed in the notebook before these features were trusted for modeling.

4.4 Encoding & scaling

Nominal categoricals (country, platform, device type, payment method, product category, return reason, shipping carrier) → StringIndexer + OneHotEncoder. customer_segment → ordinal map (New<Bronze<Silver<Gold<Platinum), since loyalty tiers have a natural order a one-hot encoding would discard. Continuous features → StandardScaler — required for the distance-based K-Means model (Section 5.2); tree models don't need it but reuse the same pipeline for consistency. 75/25 train/test split, seed=42: 45,112 train / 14,888 test rows.

5 · Machine Learning (Phase 3)

Three independent PySpark MLlib approaches — classification, clustering, and regression — implemented in notebooks/02_ml_modeling.ipynb, one per named sub-problem in the project's own scope and exceeding the brief's ≥2-approach requirement.

5.1 Approach 1 — Multi-class classification (return fraud)

Random Forest (200 trees, maxDepth=10, class-weighted) vs. multinomial Logistic Regression baseline, on abuse_label. Spark MLlib's GBTClassifier only supports binary classification — not usable for this 4-class target — so Random Forest, which natively supports multi-class, is the primary model.

Does the model earn its complexity?

Before trusting a 200-tree Random Forest, it should beat what a fraud analyst could build in an afternoon with no ML at all — an OR-rule over four already-known red flags (tracking_number_valid, refund_to_different_account, multiple_accounts_flag, address_change_before_delivery), framed as the same binary "does this return deserve a second look" decision an audit team actually makes:

Cost-sensitive threshold analysis

The default prediction picks whichever class has the highest probability — an implicit cutoff that treats every misclassification as equally costly. It isn't: a false "Legitimate → Fraudulent Return" flag burns customer trust, while a missed fraud case is a direct dollar loss. Sweeping the decision threshold on the Random Forest's Fraudulent Return probability specifically (the costliest class, per Section 1's dollar-exposure analysis) turns "re-threshold asymmetrically" from a recommendation into a concrete decision surface:

Precision stays high across the whole sweep, but recall degrades past ~0.7 — the point where a stricter cutoff starts costing caught fraud faster than it buys back precision. Full 9-point sweep in docs/threshold-analysis-results.csv; the sweep methodology, not this specific threshold, is what should carry over to production, since real data will sit at a different point on this curve.

Model-evaluation caveat (see also docs/fraud-classification-results.md): weighted F1 of 0.998–0.999 is unrealistically high for a live fraud operation. Feature importance is spread across ~15 features with no single dominant leak — this isn't a one-column shortcut — but this is a Kaggle synthetic dataset, and synthetic label generators typically compose the target from a fairly clean rule, producing near-separable classes real transactional data never gives you. We present this as proof of the pipeline and methodology: the architecture, feature engineering, and evaluation approach transfer to production; this specific accuracy number does not. A realistic production target is 80–90% weighted F1, and we recommend budgeting for that expectation, not this one, in any business case.

5.3 Approach 3 — Regression (return probability, reframed honestly)

Why not literally "predict P(this order gets returned)": every row in this dataset is already a return — there is no non-returning-order population to compare against, so the literal return-probability question is not answerable from this data without fabricating a negative class we don't have. The operationally useful analog is answerable: predict the dollar size of the refund exposure the moment a return is filed, before any manual audit happens — directly useful for finance/reserve accounting and for prioritizing high-dollar returns regardless of fraud status. This gives the project a genuine third analytical approach, satisfying the "return probability" naming in its own scope honestly rather than leaving it unaddressed.

Leakage guard: refund_to_order_value_ratio is algebraically refund_amount_requested_usd / avg_order_value_usd — an exact derivative of the target — so it is excluded from this model's features, along with the target itself (which was a legitimate feature for the classification model in 5.1, predicting abuse type, but would be pure leakage here).

Naive: refund = order value (no ML)18.6313.88—

An honest, slightly unexpected result: Linear Regression beats GBTRegressor here — the reverse of Section 5.1, where the more complex model won. avg_order_value_usd alone drives 92.5% of feature importance (see docs/regression-feature-importance.csv): refund amount is close to linear in order value, so a linear model fits that shape better than a tree ensemble tuned for non-linear interactions. The naive "refund = order value" baseline is a real bar, not a strawman — Linear Regression cuts its error by 40% (RMSE $18.63 → $11.11), while GBTRegressor barely clears it. The lesson we're stating explicitly: model complexity should match the problem's actual shape, not default to "more complex is better" — unlike Section 5.1's classifier, where Random Forest's non-linear splits earned their keep, this target is genuinely close to linear and a simpler model wins honestly.

6 · Business Insights

What the three models, taken together, actually tell us — before translating any of it into action in Section 7.

  1. Non-legitimate returns are disproportionately expensive, not just disproportionately frequent. 29.9% of orders carry 43.8% of refund dollars ($4.64M); Fraudulent Return claims average 3.1× a legitimate return.
  2. The classifier earns its complexity. Random Forest reaches perfect precision/recall on the binary "needs a second look" framing where a free 4-flag heuristic tops out at F1 0.61 (Section 5.1) — though the 0.999 multi-class F1 itself is a synthetic-data artifact, not a production expectation.
  3. The quality problem is broad-based, not one bad vendor. The Section 5.2 deep-dive found quality-issue rates banded tightly (31.5%–35.2%) across all 12 product categories — Cluster 1's 38% quality-issue share is a systemic pattern hiding inside the "legitimate" majority, not a single vendor escalation waiting to be found.
  4. A simpler model wins honestly on refund-exposure sizing. Linear Regression beats both the naive "refund = order value" baseline (40% RMSE reduction) and the more complex GBTRegressor (Section 5.3) — refund amount is close to linear in order value, and forcing a non-linear model onto a linear relationship doesn't help.
  5. The dataset's class prevalence is realistically calibrated, even if separability isn't. Our combined Fraudulent Return + Wardrobing rate (17.9%) sits close to Appriss Retail's industry-reported 15.14% fraud rate (Section 1) — the scale of the problem this project targets is real, even though the classifier's near-perfect accuracy on this specific dataset is not one to expect on live data.

7 · Strategic Recommendations

  1. Require photo evidence above a value threshold — ship this before the model. The "high-value item + no photo evidence" pattern alone touches $1.88M of non-legitimate refund exposure (4,673 orders) and needs no ML: a policy rule, live immediately, independent of the classifier's rollout timeline.
  2. Deploy the fraud classifier as a triage score, not an auto-block. Route the highest fraud_signal_score decile plus Cluster 0 members to manual fraud review; auto-approve the lowest-risk tail. Re-validate precision/recall on live traffic before trusting this dataset's 0.999 F1 — budget for 80–90% in the initial production SLA. Prioritize Fraudulent Return claims specifically: at $434.73 average vs. $141.14 for legitimate returns, each one caught is worth ~3× a typical legitimate claim in review effort saved.
  3. Give Cluster 1's 38% quality-issue rate to QA as a systemic review, not a vendor hunt. Section 6's finding that quality-issue rates are broad-based (31.5%–35.2% across every category) means the fix is a general QA/packaging/description-accuracy audit, not a search for one bad vendor — Tools (35.2%) and Electronics (34.2%) are the highest-rate starting points if a sequenced rollout is needed.
  4. Tighten policy, don't investigate, for Cluster 2. High order/return volume, low order value, Policy-Abuser-dominated — a shorter return window or restocking fee targets this segment more precisely than a fraud investigation would.
  5. Re-threshold before deployment, using the Section 5.1 sweep. A false "Legitimate → Fraudulent Return" flag costs more in customer trust than a slow manual review costs in ops time — the precision/recall trade-off across thresholds 0.1–0.9 is already mapped; picking a production threshold needs real cost estimates, not a re-run of the analysis.
  6. Feed the refund-exposure regressor to Finance, not just Fraud & Risk. Section 5.3's Linear Regression prices expected refund dollars the moment a return is filed — a reserve- accounting input independent of whether the return turns out to be fraud, abuse, or genuine.
  7. Re-validate quarterly. Fraud patterns adapt to whatever the model learns; without a temporal holdout in this dataset, we cannot measure drift here — production deployment needs a rolling time-based validation window from day one.

Risks & future roadmap

  • Risk — synthetic-data overfit to production: mitigated by treating this model as a pipeline proof-of-concept and re-training on real labeled fraud outcomes before go-live.
  • Risk — no ground-truth fraud labels in production: real retailers rarely have a clean abuse_type column; roadmap includes a human-in-the-loop labeling process from the manual review queue this system creates.
  • Risk — broad-based quality findings need root-cause work we didn't do. Section 6's quality deep-dive tells us the problem isn't one vendor, but not why defect rates are banded around 33% everywhere — that's a follow-up analysis (packaging audit, supplier QA sampling), not something this dataset can answer on its own.
  • Future: real-time scoring at return-request time (not batch), a feedback loop from review-team overrides back into re-training, extending clustering features with unstructured support-ticket text once the Flume/API ingestion (Section 3) is live, and retraining the refund- exposure regressor as a monitoring signal for reserve-accounting accuracy over time.
